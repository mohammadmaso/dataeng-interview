## 🎯 TL;DR

> A salary offer is a **package**, not one number. Your job is to understand the package, find a defensible range from evidence, and negotiate on the parts that matter to you, at the right time, with clear words.

- **Research first.** Ask people in similar roles, use job posts, and check your own experience level. Never negotiate from a feeling.
- **Look at the whole package:** base salary, bonus, supplementary insurance, loans or advances, remote-work terms, training budget, equity, and the review schedule.
- **Timing matters.** Ask about compensation after the company has shown real interest, and before you accept. Do not reveal your current number if you can avoid it.
- **Inflation changes the meaning of any raise.** A raise below inflation is a pay cut in real terms.
- **Counter with a reason, not a threat.** A written counter-offer with a clear basis works better than an ultimatum.
- This lesson gives a **method**, not market numbers. Your figures must come from your own research, at the time you negotiate.

## 🌱 Start simple (Junior)

> **💡 Analogy:** Buying a car. The sticker price is only one line. Engine warranty, insurance, fuel economy and the trade-in value all change what you really pay. A good buyer compares whole offers, not one number.

Here is the simplest version of the method:

1. **Know the range.** Find out what people with your skills and experience usually earn for similar roles. Use at least three sources.
2. **Know your package.** List every item in the offer, not only the base salary.
3. **Know your priorities.** What do you need most: more money, insurance for your family, remote days, a faster review?
4. **Ask once, clearly.** Make one specific, polite request with a reason.
5. **Decide in writing.** Confirm the final package in writing before you accept.

Here is a weak and a strong way to respond to the first offer. Note that no numbers are given, because you must fill them from your research.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Is this the best you can do?" | "Thank you, I am excited about the role. Based on the scope we discussed and what I see for similar data engineering roles, I would like to discuss the base salary. Could we look at a higher number?" |
| "I need more money, my rent went up." | "My priority is the base salary and the review timing. Could you tell me how reviews work, and whether the base can move at the first review?" |
| "I will think about it." (and never answers) | "I need until Thursday to make a decision. Can you confirm whether the package can change?" |

## 🔧 How it really works (Mid)

### Step 1: Research the range

Your evidence is the basis of every request. Build a small table with at least three rows from different sources:

| Source | What it tells you | How to weigh it |
|---|---|---|
| Ask people in the same role and level | the real current range | high, if you can confirm the role and level |
| Job posts (with a range) | what employers publish | medium; ranges are often broad |
| Recruiters and your network | market signals and what is normal | medium; check the source's incentives |
| Company reputation and size | how generous the employer tends to be | low to medium; a guess until you check |

Adjust for **level** (junior, mid, senior), for **the scope** of the job (one pipeline or a whole platform), and for **the skills in demand** (for example, streaming, lakehouse operations, or on-prem experience). Write the range as "I see a range from X to Y for this scope" only when you have real numbers.

### Step 2: Package the components

A package has parts that behave differently:

- **Base salary:** the number that compounds. Every future raise and bonus percentage uses it, so a higher base is worth more over time.
- **Bonus:** often a percentage of base or a fixed amount; check whether it is guaranteed or depends on company results.
- **Supplementary insurance:** health or family coverage beyond what the law requires. Ask for the actual coverage list, not a promise.
- **Loans or advances:** an employer-backed loan can be valuable, but it is debt. Compare the interest and the conditions.
- **Remote or hybrid work:** days per week, equipment, and internet allowance. For some people this is worth more than base salary.
- **Training and conferences:** a budget for courses or events can be a real career asset.
- **Equity or profit share:** only worth a lot if you understand the vesting, the valuation and the conditions for exit or payout. Ask in writing.
- **Review schedule:** when the next review happens and what criteria it uses.

Calculate a simple **total yearly value** that includes the parts you can estimate. Write each item with its unit and its uncertainty; an item you cannot estimate should be marked as unknown, not as zero.

```text
Offer A (example units, not market data)
  base (per month)            = 100
  yearly base                 = 100 x 12 = 1200
  bonus (guaranteed part)     = 0
  remote days (worth to me)   = 2 per week  -> high priority
  supplementary insurance     = covers family   -> high priority
  first review                = 12 months     -> ask to move to 6

Offer B
  base (per month)            = 110
  yearly base                 = 110 x 12 = 1320
  bonus (not guaranteed)      = unknown
  remote                      = office only
  supplementary insurance     = basic only
  first review                = 6 months
```

Looking only at the base line says B is better (1320 vs 1200). The table shows that A has a benefit you value more. The method is to decide which items you value, not to pick the largest number.

### Step 3: Timing

- **Do not start with compensation** in the first screen. You have little leverage until the employer has decided you are the right candidate.
- **Wait for the offer,** or the explicit signal that an offer is coming. Ask then: "What is the package you have in mind?"
- **Do not give your current salary as the anchor** if you can avoid it. Explain the range you have researched and the scope you would own.
- **Ask for time** before you answer: "Thank you, I would like two working days to review the full package."

### Step 4: Negotiation mechanics

Negotiate by **items**, not by feelings. A structure that works:

1. Thank the employer and state your interest.
2. Name the items you want to change, in order of priority.
3. Give one reason for each request (scope, market, a specific skill).
4. Ask a closed question that lets them say yes or propose a compromise.
5. Confirm the final terms in writing before you accept.

> **🎤 Say this in the interview:** "I am very interested in the role. I would like to discuss two items: the base salary, which I think should reflect the platform ownership you described, and the first review, which I would like to move to six months. What flexibility is there on these?"

### Step 5: Multiple offers

If you have two or more offers, you have real leverage, but use it honestly.

- Tell each employer, in a neutral way, that you have another process at a later stage. Do not invent offers you do not have; the market is small and the lie will return.
- Compare the offers using the **same** method and the same units.
- Ask the preferred employer what it can do for the package, not only for the base. A training budget or a different review date can be easier to approve than a base increase.
- Keep the decision date of each offer in mind. Do not let one employer's deadline force a bad decision.

### Step 6: Inflation and raises

Inflation changes the real value of any number. The formula is simple:

**real change ≈ (1 + nominal raise) / (1 + inflation) − 1**

Hypothetical example (not real data): with inflation of 30% and a raise of 20%, the real change is 1.20 / 1.30 − 1 ≈ −7.7%. Your nominal salary went up, but your buying power went down.

What this means for you:

- Ask how the company adjusts salaries over time, and whether the review uses an index or a fixed percentage.
- When the market moves fast, a raise that is only "in line with last year" can still be a loss. Re-check the range every six months.
- Keep a record of your scope changes, new skills and results; they are the basis of the next request.

## 🧠 Senior depth & trade-offs

**Trade-offs in the package:**

- **High base vs benefits.** A high base is easier to compare, but benefits such as insurance and remote work can change your real costs. Compare the total, not one line.
- **Equity or profit share vs cash.** These can be large or zero. Discount them heavily unless the terms are clear and the company's finances are visible to you.
- **Negotiating hard vs keeping the relationship.** Your first year will shape the next raise. A deal that seems great can make the next review harder if the employer feels pushed.
- **Accepting a lower base for faster growth.** A role with a clear path to senior scope can be worth a lower current number, but only if the path is written down.

**Things that often go wrong:**

- **Verbal promises** about raises, promotions, or remote days that are not in the contract. Ask for written confirmation of every item that matters.
- **Unclear bonus conditions** that depend on targets you do not control.
- **Ignoring the probation period.** Check whether any benefit starts only after a probation period, and what happens during it.
- **Confusing gross and net.** Deductions and mandatory contributions change what you actually receive. Ask whether a number is before or after deductions.

**Signals a senior candidate should notice:**

- The employer does not know how reviews work. This suggests a slow or political process.
- Constant "we are a family" language with no written terms. Use the written terms as your basis.
- A very short deadline to accept. A reasonable employer gives time for a considered decision.

**What to monitor after you accept:** whether the scope you agreed on matches your daily work, whether your review date holds, and whether your range is still correct six months later. Treat your salary as a variable you manage, not a result you wait for.

**When NOT to negotiate hard:** when the role is an opportunity for a very specific experience you cannot get elsewhere and the gap is small. Make a clear, polite request, accept a fair answer, and move on.

## 🧪 Try it

This is a method lesson, so the practice is a **written plan**. Do the steps on paper before your next offer.

**Exercise 1 — Build your range.** List the three sources you will check and write, for each, one question you will ask. Then write the range you will use, with the level and scope it applies to.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

A good answer names at least one personal contact in a similar role, one job-post range, and one recruiter or community signal, each with a specific question (for example, "What scope and level does your current role correspond to?"). The range must state its level and scope; a range without these is not usable in a negotiation.

</details>

**Exercise 2 — Compare two offers.** Offer A has a lower base, a guaranteed training budget, and remote work three days a week. Offer B has a higher base, no bonus guarantee, and office-only work. Which items would you put in your comparison table, and what would you ask each employer before deciding?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Put base, bonus (guaranteed and not), training budget, remote days (including the commute cost you would save), insurance coverage, first review date, and any equity terms in the table, all in the same time unit. Ask Offer B whether the bonus has a guaranteed minimum and what criteria apply; ask Offer A whether the training budget is in the contract and how it is approved. The decision comes from which items matter most to you, not from the largest single number.

</details>

**Exercise 3 — Write a counter-offer.** You received a package and want to ask for a higher base and an earlier review. Write the message in four sentences: your interest, the two items, one reason for each, and a question.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Example: "Thank you for the offer; I am excited about the role. I would like to discuss the base salary, which I believe should reflect the platform ownership described in the scope. I would also like to ask whether the first review can be moved to six months. Is there flexibility on either point, and could you confirm the final package in writing?"

The message is specific, names no fake competing offer, gives reasons, and ends with a question that invites a yes or a compromise.

</details>

## 💬 Interview corner

**Typical question:** "What are your salary expectations?"

- **Junior answer:** "I do not know, whatever you think is fair." This gives the employer all the power.
- **Mid answer:** "I am looking for a range of X to Y based on my research for this role." This is good if X and Y come from real research.
- **Senior answer:** explains the range by level and scope, asks what the package includes, and shows that salary is one part of a total offer. It also states that the number depends on the final scope.

> **🎤 Say this in the interview:** "Based on my research for data engineers with this scope of ownership, I am looking for a base in the range I would be glad to share once I understand the full package. Could you tell me what the role includes, such as the bonus structure, the insurance coverage and the remote policy, so I can give you a precise answer?"

Note: the answer above avoids giving a number before you know the package. Replace the phrase with your own researched range only when you have it.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes

- **Negotiating without research.** A request with no basis is easy to refuse, and it makes you look unprepared.
- **Looking only at base salary.** Insurance, remote work and training can be worth more than a small difference in base.
- **Giving your current salary** as the first number, when it sets a ceiling for your offer.
- **Inventing competing offers.** The market is small; a false claim damages your reputation.
- **Accepting verbal promises.** A raise or a remote day that is not in writing may not happen.
- **Forgetting inflation.** A nominal raise below inflation reduces your real income.
- **Asking for everything at once.** A long list of demands looks like pressure. Prioritize two or three items.
- **Accepting before reading the full package.** Ask for time; a fair employer expects it.

## ✅ Check yourself (quiz)

1. You receive an offer with a higher base than another offer, but the other offer has insurance covering your family and a training budget. What is the best next step?

   - A) Accept the higher base, because it compounds
   - B) Compare both packages in the same units, including the items you value and the uncertain ones, then ask for clarification before deciding
   - C) Reject both and wait for a third offer
   - D) Tell both employers the other offer is better, to get the highest number

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B** — the base is only one part of the value. Compare all items in the same units, mark unknowns as unknown, and ask questions before you decide. Options A and D ignore the package; C adds risk without a plan.

</details>

2. Your raise is 20% and inflation is 30% (hypothetical numbers). What is the real change in your purchasing power?

   - A) +20%
   - B) About +6%
   - C) About −7.7%
   - D) Exactly 0%

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C** — real change ≈ 1.20 / 1.30 − 1 ≈ −0.077. The nominal salary rose, but buying power fell. Adding the two percentages (A or B) is a common mistake; the correct comparison uses the ratio.

</details>

3. Which opening response is strongest when a recruiter asks about your salary expectations before the offer?

   - A) "Whatever is standard for this role."
   - B) "I am looking for a number that matches the platform ownership I would have. Could you share the package range and the structure first, so I can answer precisely?"
   - C) "My current salary is the minimum I will accept."
   - D) "I do not care about money."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B** — it avoids anchoring too early, shows you are thinking about scope, and asks for information you need. A gives no information, C sets a ceiling at your current pay, and D gives up leverage.

</details>

4. **(Senior)** You have a strong offer from a company you like, and a second offer that is pending. The first company asks you to accept by Friday. What is the most professional approach, and what trade-off do you accept?

   - A) Accept Friday and withdraw from the second process
   - B) Tell the first company you are still in another process at a later stage, ask whether the deadline can move, and accept the risk that the first offer is withdrawn if you wait
   - C) Lie about the second offer to speed up the decision
   - D) Ignore the deadline and wait silently

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B** — it is honest, it asks for the time you need, and it names the real risk. Some employers will hold the offer, others will not; that is the trade-off you accept. A and D give up without asking, and C damages trust in a small market.

</details>
